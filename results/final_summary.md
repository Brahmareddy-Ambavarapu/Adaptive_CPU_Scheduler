# Final Results Summary

This document summarizes the findings from the ML-Based Adaptive CPU Scheduling Framework. The goal of this project was to determine whether a machine learning model can predict the optimal CPU scheduling algorithm (FCFS, SJF, Round Robin, or Priority) based solely on pre-execution workload characteristics, and how that prediction performs in terms of actual scheduling regret compared to an Oracle.

## 1. Dataset and Workloads
The dataset consists of CPU workloads generated from 8 distinct statistical distributions (e.g., ShortJobHeavy, PriorityHeavy, Bimodal). For each workload, exactly 10 features were extracted mathematically (e.g., `average_burst`, `short_job_ratio`). The empirical labels were generated by simulating each workload across the 4 base schedulers and selecting the one that minimized the custom Objective Score.

## 2. ML Model Selection
In Step 11, three models (Logistic Regression, Decision Tree, Random Forest) were evaluated. The **Random Forest Classifier** emerged as the strongest model due to its robust ability to capture non-linear relationships in the workload features, achieving high classification accuracy and strong F1 scores without overfitting.

## 3. Oracle Comparison & Regret
The ultimate measure of success is **Performance Regret**: `((Adaptive Score - Oracle Score) / Oracle Score) * 100`. 
Classification accuracy alone is insufficient; a model might guess "incorrectly" according to the Oracle, but if the chosen scheduler achieves a nearly identical objective score, the regret remains near 0%.

### Adaptive vs Rule-Based (Baseline Seed)
According to `performance_summary.csv`:
- **Rule-Based Adaptive Regret:** Averaged 23.86%, achieving exactly 0% regret on only 10% of workloads.
- **ML-Based Adaptive Regret:** Averaged just 3.55%, achieving exactly 0% regret on 65% of workloads, and staying within 10% of the true Oracle optimum 95% of the time.

## 4. Generalization Results (Unseen Seed 2026)
To prove the model learned underlying principles rather than memorizing the training set, we evaluated it against a strictly unseen Workload dataset:
- **ML Generalization Accuracy:** 85.0%
- **ML Average Regret:** 8.46% (with 90% of workloads having zero regret).
- **Rule-Based Average Regret:** 36.66%

The ML scheduler drastically outperformed the hardcoded heuristics on entirely novel data.

## 5. Sensitivity Experiments

### Feature Ablation
By retraining the model with specific features dropped:
- `short_job_ratio` and `average_burst` were consistently critical to maintaining low Regret. Dropping them caused Regret spikes, confirming their predictive power.

### Objective-Weight Sensitivity
When modifying the Objective Function weights used by the Oracle (e.g., making the system heavily penalize Context Switches instead of Waiting Time):
- The ML model's regret naturally increases, as it was trained under the baseline assumption. This proves the model correctly optimized for the specific, mathematically defined baseline objective rather than random chance.

### Training-Size Sensitivity
Retraining the model on logarithmically increasing subsets of data demonstrated that diminishing returns in Regret reduction occur once sufficient representative workloads exist. A very small dataset (100 workloads) leads to fragile predictions, whereas larger datasets stabilize the Regret curve near its floor.

## 6. Key Observations
1. **Regret decoupling:** High classification accuracy strongly correlates with low Regret, but they are not identical. The ML model is extremely effective at avoiding "catastrophic" scheduling choices (like FCFS on a ShortJobHeavy workload).
2. **Computational overhead:** The extraction of 10 statistical features is perfectly scalable $O(N)$ and occurs only once per workload batch.

## 7. Limitations
- The simulation operates in discrete time ticks rather than real kernel execution.
- Workloads are strictly synthetic (though generated via robust statistical distributions).
- Context switching overhead is modeled abstractly rather than via raw CPU cycle flushing.
- Real-time/I/O blocked tasks were not modeled; processes were strictly CPU-bound.
